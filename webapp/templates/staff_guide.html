Everything an editor needs to work a job in Ereri end to end and ship something a client is happy to pay for. Open this in its own tab and keep it up alongside a real job the first few times through.

1. The two-step model

Every job is two passes over the same audio, read as one continuous document rather than a stack of separate clips:

Step 1 - Swahili source
The ASR transcript (faster-whisper), corrected against what's actually said. Timing here is real - click any word and the player jumps to that exact moment.
Step 2 - English translation
The text that actually gets spoken (by the dubbed voice) and captioned. Word timing here is estimated - proportioned across the segment by character count, since there's no real per-word alignment for translated text. That's what the dashed underline under a word means.

A human edit always wins over whatever the AI produced, at every layer - a hand-corrected source, a hand-edited translation, a manually chosen voice. Nothing you type is ever silently overwritten by a later automated pass.

2. Before you touch a segment

Two tabs on the left are worth reading before you start editing, not after:

  • Client brief - what this specific client actually asked for: speaker IDs on or off, verbatim vs. clean read, whether to transcribe song lyrics, how to handle existing burned-in subs, and what to do about no-audio / wrong-language / instrumental-only content (tag and continue, or return the order to the client). If the brief says "return", use ctrl+shift+z rather than guessing your way through it.
  • Job - the route, providers, segment/fit-rate/flag counts, and two bulk actions (detect non-speech gaps; re-translate everything not yet hand-finalized). Use these for a whole-order fix, not a one-off manual edit.

3. Step 1 - the Swahili source

Fix the ASR transcript against what's actually said, not against what you'd expect someone to say. Low-opacity words are ones the model was less confident about - saving a correction for that segment brings its words back to full opacity, so a glance at the page tells you what's actually been read versus what's still an unverified guess.

A correction here doesn't automatically retranslate itself - the English in step 2 keeps whatever it already had until you either hit Re-translate from corrected source (alt+r) or edit the English by hand. A translation that's fallen behind a later source correction gets a dashed blue outline automatically in step 2, so you don't have to remember which segments you touched.

A known failure pattern to watch for: code-switched audio. A speaker mixing Swahili and English mid-sentence (common in real Kenyan speech) is a genuine, unsolved limitation of the current transcription engines - they commit to one language for a stretch of audio, so an English word dropped into a Swahili sentence often comes back transliterated as if it were Swahili (or vice versa), not flagged as wrong, just quietly mangled. It usually (not always) trips low_asr_confidence, but don't rely on the flag alone - if you hear a speaker switch languages, read that word extra carefully against the audio and correct it by ear.

4. Step 2 - the English translation

Correct the text that actually gets spoken and captioned. This is where most of an editor's real judgment call lives: does the AI's translation say what was actually meant, does it read naturally in English, and - critically, see section 9 - does it fit the time it has to be spoken in.

If you fixed the Swahili first, re-translate before hand-editing the English, so you're correcting a fresh translation of the right text instead of patching a translation of the old, wrong one.

5. Gaps, sound tags & paragraph breaks

The empty dashed boxes in either step are real gaps - a full second or more of detected non-speech (silence, music, crowd noise, laughter). Type a tag like [MUSIC], [LAUGHTER] or [APPLAUSE] into one if it needs a caption note; leave it empty if it doesn't. A gap is never spoken by the dubbed voice - it's caption metadata only.

Use ctrl+enter on a word to start a new paragraph there - the word (and everything after it, up to the next break) drops to a new line and stays there, on every future reload, not just while you're looking at it. ctrl+⌫ on a paragraph-start word removes that break. Reach for a paragraph break wherever a new sentence genuinely reads as a new thought, or wherever a new speaker starts talking (see the next section) - not after every sentence, or the transcript stops reading as prose.

6. Speaker tags

If the client brief asks for speaker IDs, type the name in caps followed by a colon at the start of a line - e.g. RIGATHI GACHAGUA: - the same way you'd label a speaker in any transcript. Ereri recognizes that pattern automatically: it stays one bold cell instead of splitting into separate words, it's never read aloud by the dubbed voice (a real voice actor doesn't say a character's name before their own line either), and captions still show it in full. You don't need a separate control for this - just type it as you would on paper, on its own line.

7. Macros, spell check & find/replace

Macros (Macros tab) are ten reusable snippets bound to ctrl+1 through ctrl+0, inserted at your cursor - good for a recurring phrase, tag, or disclaimer specific to a client. They save themselves as you type them, same as the transcript.

Spell check (step 2 only) flags possibly-misspelled words in red. Click a flagged cell for suggestions - "Replace all" fixes every matching occurrence in the segment at once, or click a single suggestion to fix just that one, or "Ignore" to dismiss it if it's actually correct (a name, a brand, deliberate slang). It checks spelling only, not grammar - read your own sentences back.

Find/replace (ctrl+shift+f) works across the whole current step - useful for a name that came back misspelled the same way in twelve different segments.

8. The flag glossary

A flagged segment gets a dotted underline instead of a callout box - hover it, or press alt+↑/↓ to jump between flagged spots, to see why. The tooltip shows the raw reason code(s) below, joined together exactly as the system recorded them - here's what each one actually means and what to do about it.

Reason codeWhat it meansWhat to do
low_asr_confidenceThe Swahili transcript itself is a shaky guess. Listen to the source audio and correct step 1 against what's actually said.
empty_transcriptThe model heard nothing at all for a segment longer than 1.5s - more serious than a shaky guess, there's no draft to proofread. Transcribe it from scratch by ear.
low_mt_confidenceThe translation itself is a low-confidence guess. Read it against the (corrected) Swahili and fix anything that's wrong or unnatural.
mt:sheng, mt:unclear, mt:numbers, mt:time_expression The translator itself flagged something specific: slang it wasn't fully sure of, an ambiguous phrase, a number, or - the highest-stakes one in Kenyan content - a time expression (East Africa often runs on "swahili time", offset from the 12-hour clock a literal translation would produce). Double-check that exact word or phrase by ear against the source.
explicit_content_flaggedA coarse keyword match against known explicit terms - not a reliable detector on its own, legitimate content (medical, news reporting on assault) can trip it too. Use your judgment on whether it's actually a problem; this exists so a human decides, not so anything gets auto-rejected.
duration_overflowThe translation is a bit too long or short for its slot (needs_shortening / needs_lengthening) but a normal-sounding time-stretch could plausibly fix it. Usually resolves itself once you save - see section 9. If it doesn't, tighten or pad the English text.
unfittableThe translation is too long or short for even an emergency stretch to rescue. Rewrite the English shorter (or, rarely, longer) - see section 9's playbook.
stretch_cap_exceededThe audio was synthesized but left un-stretched - it didn't fit in its own real estate even at the emergency stretch ceiling. This is the one that used to risk an audible overlap before the mixer fix below - it no longer can, but the segment's audio may now be cut short in the delivered mix. Rewrite the English shorter and re-synthesize (ctrl+shift+s).
emergency_stretch_appliedThe audio needed more than the normal, fully natural-sounding stretch to fit - it was stretched anyway, up to a harder ceiling, rather than being left unfittable. Give it a listen. If the pace sounds pushed, shortening the English is still the better fix than leaving it stretched.

9. Timing, stretching & why a dub used to overlap itself

What was happening: every segment's dubbed voice only ever gets its own original time slot in the source audio to work with. When a translation was too long to fit even after stretching, the mixer used to just play the next segment's audio on top of whatever was still playing from the one before it - two voices summed together, audibly talking over each other, occasionally loud enough to clip. That was a real bug in the mixer, not an inherent limit of dubbing, and it's fixed now: the mixer physically cannot place two segments' audio in the same moment any more - a segment that runs long gets its tail cut cleanly at the point the next one starts, never summed on top of it. You will never hear one voice on top of another in a delivered file again.

That fix alone trades "audible overlap" for "silently cut short", which is why the rest of this system exists - to make that cut as rare as possible, and to make sure a human always catches the segments where it still happens, before anything ships:

  1. Borrowed silence. If a segment is directly followed by a real silent gap, its translation is now allowed to run into that silence (minus a always-kept 200ms pause) instead of being confined to its own original slot. That's real dead air nobody would otherwise hear speech in - not time taken from anywhere. This alone fixes most "just a bit too long" cases for free.
  2. Normal time-stretch, up to 8%. Applied silently, no flag - this range sounds completely natural (pitch-preserving, not a chipmunk effect).
  3. Emergency stretch, up to 25%. A last resort before a segment is left unfittable - applied and flagged emergency_stretch_applied so you know to sanity-check the pace.
  4. Still too long: flagged, not overlapped. Flagged stretch_cap_exceeded / unfittable, audio placed at its natural length and hard-clipped at the mix stage if it would otherwise reach the next segment. This is exactly the worklist Ereri's flags exist for.

The actual playbook for an unfittable segment: the fix is almost always a shorter English sentence, not a timing trick - shorter synonyms ("about" instead of "regarding"), dropping filler words, cutting a subordinate clause that doesn't change the meaning, or splitting one long sentence across this segment and a directly-following one if the source audio actually has a pause there. Rewrite, save, and check the flag clears (ctrl+shift+s re-synthesizes and re-times that one segment immediately, so you don't have to wait for a full re-render to see whether your rewrite actually fixed it).

Two things this system deliberately does not attempt, so you know what not to expect from it: it doesn't duck a background track under the dubbed voice (the delivered dub is a single voice track, not a voice-over-original mix, so there's no second track to duck), and it doesn't do visual AI lip-sync (matching the video's mouth movements to the new audio) - that's a genuinely different, GPU-heavy capability this platform doesn't have built, not a setting you're missing.

10. Voice cloning & consent

A cloned voice (matching the original speaker's actual voice, rather than a standard TTS voice) only ever gets used with a real, logged consent - if that hasn't been granted, don't propose cloning as a way to fix a timing or quality issue. The reference clip is auto-extracted from the source audio's longest real speech segment, 6-20 seconds - if a job's warnings mention a short reference clip, expect a less reliable clone and say so if a client asks.

11. Caption & transcript style rules

Verbatim vs. clean read
Follow whatever the client brief specifies (Client brief tab). Clean read smooths filler words and false starts for readability; full verbatim keeps every "um", repetition and false start. When in doubt, clean read is the safer default for a general audience.
Captions on screen
Capped automatically at 6 seconds on screen and wrapped at 32 characters per line - you don't need to manage this by hand, it's applied when subtitles are generated.
Cut-off speech
Add a trailing -- (alt+d) rather than trailing off with an ellipsis or guessing the rest of the sentence.
Inaudible speech
Tag with [inaudible] (alt+n) rather than guessing at words you genuinely can't make out.
Crosstalk / interposing voices
Prefix with >> (alt+l).
Italics
Only if the client brief asks for it (on-screen text or narration) - real markup that carries into the delivered SRT/VTT, not just a visual toggle in the editor. alt+i toggles italics on one cell; ctrl+shift+i turns on italic mode - every cell you focus after that gets italicized too, useful for a whole run (a song, a foreign-language aside) without hitting alt+i on each word - press it again to turn the mode off. It never un-italicizes anything already marked.

The rest of this guide (sections 12-16) is Kauli's full transcript style guide - the detailed rules behind the quick reference above. Client-specific instructions on the Client brief tab always override anything below.

12. Transcript styles - Clean Read, Verbatim & Verbatim Light

Which style applies to a given order is set on the Client brief tab - these are what each one actually means:

Clean Read (most common)
Transcribe what's actually said, as close to the true audio as possible, while cleaning up small-scale noise: a word repeated while the speaker formulates their next point, filler words ("you know," "sort of," "um," "like"), and false starts that don't add anything. A good Clean Read transcript still contains the entire conversation - digressions, jokes, relevant sound effects or music - and keeps the speaker's exact words with standard spelling ("going to" rather than "gonna," "want to" rather than "wanna"). Cleaning up noise is not the same as omitting real content.
Verbatim (used rarely)
Capture each word exactly as spoken, with no editing for clarity - every filler word, false start, stammer and interjection included. Offset filler words with commas, put a double dash after a false start, and dashes after a stammer (whether of an initial sound or a whole word). Don't burn time trying to tell an "um" from an "uh" or counting exactly how many there were - approximate.
Verbatim Light (Entertainment & Media)
The goal is communicating the tone and meaning to the audience while keeping captions easy to follow - transcribe the spoken content only if there's enough cell space for it, given Ereri's 6-second / 32-character caption limits (see the quick reference above). This one takes judgment and practice, especially syncing a file with difficult or fast speech.

13. Editing calls - speaker errors, filler words, false starts & interjections

Speaker errors
Leave a speaker's own error as-is, in every style - the incorrect version is a better duplicate of what a real listener heard. Even when you're sure they're wrong, a "fix" can introduce a worse error you can't see: they say "Book XYZ by John Smith," you know Book XYZ is actually by Jane Doe so you change the name - except they meant John Smith's other book, AYZ, and your correction just replaced one error with a different one.
Filler words
Clean Read eliminates the extraneous ones; Verbatim keeps every one; Verbatim Light depends on available cell space. Examples: "uh," "um," "kind of," "sort of," "like," "you know," "right," "OK." These are only filler when they show up almost every sentence, make the sentence harder to read instead of clearer, or the speaker clearly isn't trying to communicate the literal phrase. A sentence that opens with "and," "but," or "so" as a conjunction is not a filler word - keep it.
Verbatim: "We sort of, you know, went all over the place with this." → Clean Read: "We sort of went all over the place with this." Both keeping and dropping the second "sort of"/"like" can be correct - use judgment, not a mechanical strip.
False starts
A false start is a repeated word or phrase (even mid-sentence) that isn't there for emphasis - tone of voice helps tell a false start from filler. Clean Read eliminates the short ones; for a false start longer than a few words, keep it and mark the change in direction with a double dash (alt+d). A self-correction can usually be omitted unless the mistake itself matters, or removing it would lose real context.
Verbatim: "It was-- it was a really great trip." → Clean Read: "It was a really great trip." But "It was a really, really great trip" needs no correction - that repetition is emphasis, not a false start.
Passive interjections
Clean Read drops pure active-listening noises like "yeah" or "mm-hmm" (common in interviews); Verbatim keeps them exactly as spoken; Verbatim Light depends on cell space.

14. Editing in Ereri - speaker IDs, sync, paragraphs, spell check & sentence length

Speaker ID judgment calls
The mechanic is in section 6 - type NAME: at the start of a line. Choosing which name is the actual skill: use the file name, description, on-screen text or a quick web search; use first and last name when you have both; if a speaker introduces themself with a title and surname only ("I'm Mrs. Jones"), use that as given, but drop a title like Mr./Dr./Ms. once you know the full name; a stage name or royal title is used as-is (MADONNA:, KING CHARLES:). If only some speakers can be identified, name the ones you can and number the rest (SPEAKER 1:, SPEAKER 2:) - with only one unnamed speaker, drop the number (SPEAKER:). For sports, use ANNOUNCER:/COMMENTATOR: when offscreen voices are hard to tell apart or there are more than three, REPORTER: for an offscreen reporter's question, and PA: for stadium loudspeaker announcements. Use INTERPRETER: only when speaker IDs are required in the first place, for someone verbally interpreting another speaker's (or a sign-language signer's) speech.
Synchronization
Ereri's cells are real per-word ASR timestamps in step 1 (estimated by character count in step 2) - the rule is the same one any timed-caption tool works to: a word belongs in whichever cell is highlighted/active when the speaker actually begins saying it. More than one word can land in the same cell if they're spoken quickly together; a long word (a big number, a drawn-out name) can span several cells - keep it all together in the first cell where it starts, rather than splitting it mid-word across cells. You can't add a brand-new cell out of nothing - alt+k splits an existing cell at your cursor, ctrl+m merges one into the next, when the ASR's original word boundaries don't match what you actually need.
Research
Confirming the spelling of a name or term is a real part of the job, not optional polish. Check the order's file name and Client brief tab first, watch the video for on-screen text if one's attached, and flag anything you can't resolve immediately so you can jump back to it (alt+f, then alt+↑/↓) once later context in the file clears it up.
Paragraph length
The mechanic is in section 5 (ctrl+enter / ctrl+⌫). Aim for roughly 3-5 sentences or 30-45 seconds per paragraph - shorter paragraphs are easier to proofread and scan. Some will run longer or shorter depending on content; look for a natural transition point rather than waiting for a full topic change if a speaker stays on one subject for a while.
Spell check: Ignore vs. Ignore all
The mechanic is in section 7. "Ignore" dismisses the flag on just the one cell you clicked; "Ignore all" is for a word that's correct everywhere in this order (a name, a brand, deliberate slang) - it clears every currently-flagged instance and remembers, so the word won't come back flagged on a later spell-check run either. Orange/red flags are just "not in our dictionary," not "wrong" - proper names and specialized terms routinely trip it; if you've researched one and know it's right, Ignore All it and move on rather than second-guessing it every time it reappears.
Sentence length
Keep sentences short - one or two clauses where possible - for both readability and caption fit. It's fine to open a sentence with a conjunction ("and," "but," "so," "for," "nor," "or," "yet"). Avoid semicolons; a period can almost always stand in for one.
Best: "He knew it, and I knew he knew it. So that's why I asked him for help and got what I needed in the end." Too long: the same sentence with the period after "it" replaced by a comma, run on to the end.

15. Grammar reference - sentence structure, common mistakes & house spellings

Independent vs. dependent clauses
An independent clause is a complete thought that can stand alone ("She went to the store"). A dependent (subordinate) clause can't stand alone and needs an independent clause to complete its meaning - it typically opens with a subordinating conjunction ("because," "although," "if," "when") or a relative pronoun ("who," "which," "that"): "Although very interesting" isn't a sentence on its own; "Although very interesting, the lecture was too long" is.
Commonly confused words
a while / awhileAlways two words: "Wait here for a while."
a lotAlways two words, never "alot."
all rightAlways two words, never "alright."
anyway / any wayOne word as an adverb ("Anyway, let's move on"); two as an adjective-noun phrase ("any way possible").
complimentary / complementaryFree/nice ("complimentary tickets") vs. fitting together well ("complementary colors").
everyday / every dayOne word as an adjective ("my everyday shoes"); two words for frequency ("I go there every day").
Google / googleCapitalized as the brand; lowercase as the verb ("I needed to google that").
it's / its"It's" only ever means "it is." The possessive is "its," no apostrophe.
queue / cueA line of people or a software queue vs. a signal to start, or a pool stick.
sight / site / citeVision; a place (incl. a website); to reference a source.
wary / wearyCautious vs. tired.
who's / whose"Who is" vs. the possessive of "who."
All spelling is American English, except a proper name spelled its own way (an organization called the "Colour Centre" keeps its own spelling). A product name that's also a common noun still pluralizes the way it's commonly used online, even against normal rules: "a few BlackBerrys," "a few Nissan Leafs."
House spellings

Kauli's preferred form where a word has more than one correct spelling:

  • Abbreviations: 2D, 3D, B2B, mic/miked up, MOOC, OK (never okay/O.K.), IDed (never ID'd), till
  • Lowercase: euro, eurozone, googleability, to google (the verb), internet
  • Single word, no hyphen: antisemitism, dropdown, ebook, email, offscreen, onscreen, plugin, smartphone, smartwatch, website
  • Two words: a lot, all right, data set, et cetera (never etc.), health care, home page, more so, web page
  • Spellings: advisor (not adviser), free rein (not free reign), versus (not vs.) - "Z" is always written as Z/z regardless of how a speaker pronounces it ("zed")
  • Proper nouns: JavaScript, PowerPoint, Quran (not Koran), US (no periods), YouTube, eBay, ExxonMobil, Google+/Paramount+ (not "Plus"), 9/11 when said as "nine-eleven" but "September 11" when said in full, al-Qaeda

16. Punctuation reference

Keep punctuation as simple as the sentence allows, in service of what the speaker is actually saying. Always acceptable: apostrophes, commas, double dashes, hyphens, periods, question marks. Occasionally acceptable: ampersands, at symbols, colons, em dashes, hashtags. Semicolons and parentheses are never used. If something here doesn't cover a real case you hit, flag the cell for QA and check the finalized transcript in your Account History afterward for how it was actually handled.

Kauli's rule: always write "dot dot slash" out as words. Never type the literal characters ../ into a transcript or caption.

Ampersands (&)
Only for company/brand names and set terms that are actually written with one: "Johnson & Johnson," "R&D," "AT&T." Spell out "and" everywhere else.
Apostrophes
Write a possessive plural ending in S the way the speaker actually pronounces it - "Dickens' novel" or "Dickens's novel," whichever extra syllable you actually hear. "It's" always means "it is"; the possessive "its" never takes an apostrophe.
At symbols (@)
Social handles and email addresses only: @username, john.doe@example.com.
Colons
Restrict colons to after a speaker ID, or where one's structurally part of the text (a URL, a time like "8:00," a biblical citation like "Matthew 7:3"). Anywhere you'd otherwise reach for a colon as punctuation, use a double dash or a comma instead.
Commas
Oxford comma in a list of three or more ("milk, eggs, and bread"); before a coordinating conjunction joining two independent clauses ("I want to go, but I have to study"); after an introductory adverbial phrase longer than three words (required) or three words or fewer (optional); after an introductory/exclamatory word ("Oh, you're right!"); around a mid-sentence interjection; between adjectives modifying the same noun; around the year in a full date and around a state/country in a city-state pair. Watch for: a comma splice (joining two independent clauses with just a comma - use a period instead); comma clutter (a comma before every "and" in a list); and misusing a comma with a nonrestrictive clause (offset it: "The professor, who had years of experience, gave a great lecture") versus a restrictive one (no commas: "The students who finished the extra work earned an A" - it specifies which students).
Double dashes
alt+d appends one. Always two dashes, never one (a single dash is only for a hyphenated word) and never an em dash. Use it when a sentence changes direction mid-thought, when a speaker is cut off or interrupts themself with an aside, or (Verbatim only) for a stammer or repeated word. The word right after a double dash isn't capitalized unless it normally would be without the dash there.
Exclamation points
Use sparingly in general content (presentations, interviews, lectures) for real emphasis or excitement; entertainment content (movies, sports, reality TV) can carry them more liberally since conveying the on-screen emotion is the actual goal there.
Hashtags
When a speaker says "hashtag" plus a word, write the pound symbol with the tag: "hashtag videoSEO" → #videoSEO. Always hashtag a reference to the #MeToo movement even if the word "hashtag" itself isn't spoken.

17. Dubbing - speaker IDs & formatting

This section only applies to an order that produces a dub - a real voice track gets synthesized from whatever's tagged with each speaker ID. For a transcription/caption-only order, sections 6 and 14's general speaker ID guidance is all that applies.

A speaker ID must be genuinely unique, for the whole file
Once you've assigned an ID to a speaker, never reuse it for a different person, even much later in the same file. This isn't just a readability convention here - the Job tab's Speakers panel assigns exactly one dub voice per distinct speaker_id string (distinct_speakers = sorted({s.speaker_id for s in job.segments if s.speaker_id}) - a plain set of the literal strings, nothing fuzzier). Reuse an ID for two different people and Ereri can't tell them apart - both get the same dub voice, silently, with nothing on screen to warn you it happened.
Named speakers
Use the full name where it's known or reasonably determinable from the file. If two speakers share a first name and you don't have last names, number the first name rather than leaving them ambiguous: JOHN 1: / JOHN 2:.
Unnamed speakers
Prefer a role-based ID over a bare generic one wherever the file actually supports it - REPORTER:, DOCTOR:, OFFICER:, DRIVER:, STUDENT: - falling back to SPEAKER:/MAN:/WOMAN: only when no role can be inferred. Keep an ID as short as it can be while staying unambiguous - OFFICER, not OFFICER OF THE COURT; 911, not 911 OPERATOR.
Always number multiple unidentified speakers - no exception past seven
Section 14's general speaker ID guidance allows dropping numbers on unnamed speakers once there are more than seven, when numbering would confuse a human reader more than it helps. For a dub, don't do that: keep a number on every distinct unidentified speaker no matter how many there are, since each number is a real, separate voice slot, not just a reading aid - two unnumbered SPEAKER: tags for two different real people collapse to one voice between them, exactly like the point above.
An ID stays the same for that person for the whole file
If MAN 1 goes quiet for a long stretch and comes back later, he's still MAN 1 - re-tagging him under a new number at that point would hand him a new, different dub voice for no reason.
Formatting for a dub's text specifically
Don't transcribe background lyrics into a segment headed for dubbing, even if the order's brief otherwise asked for song lyrics elsewhere - music playing underneath dialogue isn't something a dub voice should ever end up trying to sing. And never type a parenthetical stage direction into a segment's text - (ON PHONE), (VOICEOVER), (WHISPERING), (SARCASTICALLY) and the like. Ereri already strips a [bracketed] tag like [MUSIC] before it reaches the TTS engine - captioned, never spoken (strip_bracket_tags_for_tts in kauli/pipeline.py) - but that function only matches square brackets, not parentheses. Type (ON PHONE) into a segment and the dub voice will synthesize and speak those words aloud, literally. Leave the direction out of the text entirely - if it matters to a caption reader, a bracket tag is the only form that's actually safe to use.

18. Saving, approving & the 100%-edited gate

Everything saves itself - a segment autosaves a couple of seconds after you stop typing, and the topbar always shows whether everything's saved. ctrl+s forces it immediately; ctrl+shift+s also re-renders that segment's audio.

"Approve & mark ready for delivery" is a hard gate, not a suggestion: an order cannot ship until every real speech segment (gaps don't count) has been saved at least once in step 2 - even a segment that needed no changes has to be touched once so it registers as reviewed. The staff queue's "% Edited" column tracks this in real time; the approve action itself will refuse and tell you exactly what's still outstanding if you try before it hits 100%. There is no way to bypass this from the editor - it's the platform's actual promise that nothing ships un-reviewed.

20. If Ereri seems broken

Tabs not switching, macros not responding, shortcuts not firing - almost always a stale cached copy of the editor's script fighting a newer page underneath it. Hard refresh first (ctrl+shift+r, or ctrl+F5) before assuming something's actually broken - it fixes the large majority of "nothing's responding" reports.

If a hard refresh doesn't fix it, or something looks genuinely wrong with the data (missing audio, a translation that doesn't match the source, a flag that doesn't clear after a real fix), say so directly rather than working around it - see below.
