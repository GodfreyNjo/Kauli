## PART 1: THE RIGHT ORDER (1:45 to 4:00)

[CHAPTER 01:45, The right order]

[SFX: soft page turn]

AMARA: Dan, where do people go wrong first?

DAN: Order. They start with the last step. They run the video through a dubbing tool, listen to the result, and then they discover the transcript underneath was wrong. So now there's a wrong script, a wrong translation, and a wrong voice, all stacked on one tiny mistake.

AMARA: And the fix is to go in order.

DAN: Always the same four steps. One, a clean transcript in the original language. Two, a translation of that transcript. Three, subtitles built from the translation. Four, only if you want it, a dub built from the same translation.

AMARA: Why that order? Why not translate straight from the audio?

DAN: Because every step takes its input from the one before. If the transcript says "fourteen clients" and the speaker said "forty", then every language you make from it says fourteen. But if you fix it once, at the start, every language gets fixed at the same time.

AMARA: So the transcript is the single source of truth.

DAN: It is. Fix it once, in the language you actually speak, where you can check it. Then everything flows from there.

AMARA: And that's a nice bit of luck, because the original language is the one you can check.

DAN: Exactly. You can't check a Swahili subtitle if you don't read Swahili. But you can check the English transcript it came from. So put your effort where you can see.

AMARA: What about the picture? Does the video matter?

DAN: One thing. Before you translate, look for text that's already on screen. A name, a slide, a lower third. Subtitles in a new language will want to sit at the bottom, and they'll cover it. Plan for that now, not after.

AMARA: So, step zero is to watch your own video once, with the sound off, and write down what's on screen.

DAN: Two minutes. Saves an hour.

## PART 2: WRITE AND SPEAK FOR TRANSLATION (4:00 to 6:30)

[CHAPTER 04:00, Write and speak so it survives translation]

[SFX: soft page turn]

AMARA: Let's go back to the backpack. "It's a steal." Why did that break?

DAN: Because it's an idiom. The meaning isn't in the words. It's in a shared habit. "A steal" means a bargain, but only to people who share that habit. Translation tools work from the words and the context, and an idiom gives them almost no context to work with.

AMARA: So the first rule: speak plainly, if you can.

DAN: If you know a video will be translated, say what you mean. "It's a great price." "It's very cheap for what you get." Save the jokes and the sayings for the version that's only for your home audience.

AMARA: What else breaks?

DAN: Numbers, dates and units. "Three four twenty-six" is the third of April in one country and the fourth of March in another. Say the month out loud. "Fifty miles" means nothing to half the world, so say the kilometres as well. Currency too. Say "twenty US dollars", not "twenty bucks".

AMARA: Brand names and product names.

DAN: Yes. Decide which names must never be translated. Your company, your product, a person's name, a place. Write them in a short list, and give the list to whoever, or whatever, is translating. That one list prevents more embarrassing mistakes than anything else on this page.

AMARA: And "Aurora backpack" doesn't become "Dawn knapsack".

DAN: [laughs] It really can. Machines love to be helpful.

AMARA: Pace. Does it matter how fast I talk?

DAN: A lot. Fast speech with overlap and filler words is harder for the transcription, and the errors travel into every language. Speak a little slower than feels natural. Leave a short pause between ideas. And one voice at a time, we said that last time and we'll keep saying it.

AMARA: Anything about humour?

DAN: Humour is the most local thing there is. If a joke matters, check how it comes out in the other language, with a person who reads it. If it doesn't survive, cut it from that version. A missing joke is better than a strange one.

## PART 3: SUBTITLES IN A NEW LANGUAGE (6:30 to 9:00)

[CHAPTER 06:30, Subtitles in a new language]

[SFX: soft page turn]

AMARA: Okay. We have a clean transcript and a good translation. Now the subtitles. Is this just the same subtitle file with new words in it?

DAN: No, and that's the trap. Languages take up different amounts of room. A sentence in Spanish or German is often a fifth or a quarter longer than the English. A sentence in Chinese is much shorter on the page, because each character carries more. So the timing and the line breaks that worked in English can fail in the new language.

AMARA: So the lines get too long.

DAN: Or they flash by too fast. A viewer needs time to read. A safe guide is two lines at most, short lines, and long enough on screen to read comfortably. If the translated line is too long to read in the time, shorten it. A good subtitle is a summary of the speech, not a transcript of it.

AMARA: That feels like a big change from how we talked about captions in English.

DAN: It's the same idea, with more pressure. In your own language, a subtitle that stays and tells people exactly what was said can be fine. In another language, you're doing two jobs: translate, and fit.

AMARA: And line breaks.

DAN: Break at a natural pause, not in the middle of a phrase. Keep a name together. And keep the subtitles out of the way of anything already on screen.

AMARA: A word about scripts and directions.

DAN: Some languages are written right to left, like Arabic and Hebrew, and some need a font that supports their characters. Test the file in the actual player before you publish. Open it, and watch thirty seconds. That's the one test nobody regrets.

AMARA: And the file format.

DAN: SRT works nearly everywhere. VTT is the web standard. Pick the one your platform asks for. And keep the original-language file too, so you can fix both when something changes.

## PART 4: SUBTITLES OR A DUB? (9:00 to 10:45)

[CHAPTER 09:00, Subtitles or a dub?]

[SFX: soft page turn]

AMARA: So this is the question I hear most. Subtitles or a dub?

DAN: Let me give you three questions. First: how do your viewers watch? On a phone, with the sound off, on a commute? Subtitles. Lean back on a sofa, or doing something else while they listen? A dub.

AMARA: Second?

DAN: How much is on the screen? If people need to look at a diagram, a demonstration, a product, the picture is doing the work. A dub lets them look. Subtitles ask them to read and look at the same time, and that's tiring.

AMARA: And third?

DAN: Who is the audience? Children, people who read slowly, people who read a different script, or people who just prefer to listen. A dub reaches them. And on the other side, someone who is deaf or hard of hearing needs captions, whatever you decide to do with the voice. So very often the best answer is both. A dub, and subtitles on top.

AMARA: What about cost and effort?

DAN: A dub is more work to get right, because you're making a voice and fitting it in time. Subtitles are quicker to make and easier to fix. So if you're not sure, start with subtitles in your top two languages, see which videos do well, and dub the ones that earn it.

AMARA: That's a lovely, calm way to do it.

DAN: Prove it with the cheap step first.

## PART 5: DUBBING WITHOUT EMBARRASSMENT (10:45 to 13:00)

[CHAPTER 10:45, Dubbing without embarrassment]

[SFX: soft page turn]

AMARA: Let's talk about dubs. What should people expect, honestly?

DAN: Honestly: a dubbed voice reads the translated script in a new language, and it's timed to fit your video. A good one sounds natural, and it saves you a studio. But it's a new recording of a translation. It's not the original person speaking.

AMARA: And lips?

DAN: This is the one limit worth knowing. A dub doesn't change the speaker's mouth. If the camera is close on someone's face, viewers will see the difference. For a talking head, that may be fine, and many people are comfortable with it. For a close-up of an emotional moment, you may prefer subtitles. Test one clip before you commit to fifty.

AMARA: Length.

DAN: The new language has a different length, remember. A dub has to fit the time available, so the script sometimes needs to be tighter than the literal translation. If a dub sounds rushed, the script is usually too long, not the voice too fast.

AMARA: The voice itself.

DAN: Pick a voice that suits the content and keep it the same all the way through. A friendly voice for a how-to. A calm one for a training video. And listen to the whole thing, not just the first ten seconds.

AMARA: And, importantly, voice cloning.

DAN: Yes. If a dub is going to sound like the original speaker, that's a person's voice, and it needs their consent. Ask first. Get it in writing. Say what it will be used for. And if you're working with a service, check that it asks you to confirm that you have the right, before it clones anything.

AMARA: Music and sound effects.

DAN: If you have a separate music track, keep it separate and put it back under the new voice. If the music is baked into the audio, test it, because the voice and the music have to share the space.

AMARA: And the rule I'd add: have someone who speaks the language listen.

DAN: Always. Even for ten minutes. One native listener catches what nothing else will.

## THE CHEAT SHEET (14:30 to 15:15)

[MUSIC: bed in, soft and upbeat]

AMARA: Here are the ten points. Screenshot them.

DAN: One. Fix the transcript first, in your own language. Two. Translate from the fixed transcript, never from the audio. Three. Look at what's on screen before you start.

AMARA: Four. Speak plainly. Skip idioms, and say the month, the units and the currency. Five. Keep a list of names that must not be translated.

DAN: Six. Expect subtitles to need re-timing, because languages differ in length. Seven. Test the file in the real player.

AMARA: Eight. Subtitles when people watch with the sound off, a dub when they listen, and often both. Nine. Test one dubbed clip, especially close-ups, before you do the whole catalogue.

DAN: And ten. Get a person who speaks the language to listen, and get consent before any voice is cloned.
